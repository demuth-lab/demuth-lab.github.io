## Questions we ask
- How does selection for increased male horn size generate correlated costs in female abdomen size and reproductive capacity?
- What genetic mechanisms maintain sexually antagonistic selection in shared genomes?
- How do loser effects following male-male contests alter behavior and physiology?
- Can social defeat in beetles provide a useful model for understanding stress-induced behavioral changes relevant to PTSD?

## What we do
- Experimental evolution and quantitative genetics in *Gnatocerus cornutus*
- Study of sexually antagonistic selection on exaggerated male horn size and correlated female traits
- Behavioral assays of male-male contests and loser effects
- Neurogenetic and genomic analyses of stress-responsive behavioral change

## Why it matters
Traits that improve fitness in one sex can impose costs in the other, creating persistent evolutionary conflict. At the same time, social defeat can leave lasting effects on behavior that parallel broader questions about stress susceptibility and resilience. By using the broad-horned flour beetle as a model, this research connects the evolution of morphology and sexual conflict with the biological basis of social stress.
